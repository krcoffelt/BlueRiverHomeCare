Start with one ordinary day, the person’s preferences, and the help family can realistically provide. You do not need to decide every care task or buy a package before asking for help. This checklist turns scattered concerns into a useful first conversation.

[Open the printable planning worksheet](/downloads/home-care-planning-checklist.html), then use your browser’s Print command. It has space for handwritten notes and a family responsibility table. Nothing you write on the printed sheet is submitted to Blue River.

## Short on time? Start with these three lines

Before calling, write one sentence for each prompt:

1. **What changed:** “Dad is leaving laundry unfinished because the stairs have become difficult.”
2. **What help would be welcome:** “He would like help with laundry while keeping his usual morning routine.”
3. **When family cannot help:** “We can visit on weekends, but weekday afternoons are uncovered.”

These are examples, not a suggested care package. The rest of the checklist helps you turn your own observations into questions and a realistic next step.

## 1. Begin with what your loved one wants to keep

Ask, “Which parts of your day would you most like to keep doing your own way?” That might be choosing meals, tending a favorite plant, visiting with a friend, or having a quiet morning. Record those preferences before listing problems.

Make the conversation specific and respectful. “Would help getting lunch ready make afternoons easier?” is more actionable than “You need someone here.” Invite the older adult to help decide what support would feel welcome and what boundaries matter.

Use three prompts:

- I want to keep doing: ____________________
- I would welcome help with: ____________________
- I am uncomfortable with or want to discuss: ____________________

If the family and the older adult see things differently, write both perspectives down. The first conversation does not have to settle every disagreement.

## 2. Describe the routine instead of guessing the service

Write what happens in the morning, afternoon, and evening. Note what the person does independently, where someone already helps, and what remains uncovered. Describe a recent example rather than giving a broad label such as “not managing.”

For example: “Lunch is prepared when my sister visits on Tuesdays and Thursdays. The other weekdays are uncertain.” That observation gives the family and provider a clearer question to discuss than “needs meal help.”

Consider these everyday routines:

- **Meals:** What needs preparing, and what preferences should be respected?
- **Companionship:** When would conversation or a familiar activity be welcome?
- **Laundry and light cleaning:** Which tasks are difficult to keep up with?
- **Moving around at home:** What help is already used, and what concerns need assessment?
- **Family involvement:** Who checks in, visits, or manages arrangements now?

Blue River’s confirmed [in-home caregiving services](/services/in-home-caregiving/) include practical daily support. If a familiar presence is the main goal, explore [companion care](/services/companion-care/). The specific tasks still need to be agreed on with the client, family, and provider.

This worksheet is for non-medical planning. Bring medical treatment, new symptoms, and medication questions to the appropriate health professional. Our [home care versus home health guide](/resources/home-care-vs-home-health-kansas-city/) explains the distinction.

## 3. Mark the times when help is missing

A list of tasks becomes more useful when you add timing. Start with the gaps you already know rather than assuming a number of weekly care hours.

Write down:

- The days and times when family cannot be present.
- The routines that need help at those times.
- Which times are preferences and which are essential.
- Any work, travel, or family commitments that change the schedule.
- The date you hope to begin, and whether it is flexible.

For example, “Monday lunch preparation is uncovered” is more useful than requesting a generic morning visit. Ask the provider how the proposed schedule would address the actual gap. Confirm minimum visits, current availability for the address, and any scheduling terms before relying on a plan. Office opening hours do not establish available care shifts.

## 4. Give every family task a person and a backup

Avoid leaving responsibilities under “we will handle it.” Ask each person what they can sustain, including the time and travel involved. A willing relative may be able to coordinate calls but not make a midday visit.

The National Institute on Aging provides worksheets for coordinating caregiving responsibilities. Its practical starting point is to identify tasks and share them; our printable worksheet applies that idea to your first planning conversation.

For each task, record the main person, timing, and backup. Include tasks outside paid visits, such as organizing appointments, checking supplies, and handling provider communication. An unanswered line is useful: it reveals a gap the family still needs to solve.

Agree on one primary contact for the provider, with the older adult’s permission and involvement. Ask how updates, questions, and changes should be communicated. Do not assume a particular app, daily report, or response time is included unless the provider confirms it.

## 5. Prepare questions about fit, cost, and changes

Bring a short question list so everyone hears the same answers:

- Which of the tasks we described can you provide, and which need a different professional?
- Is care available for this address and the times we need?
- What visit minimums, rates, and additional charges apply?
- What happens when the client or caregiver needs to change a visit?
- How do we raise a concern or request a change to the plan?
- Who should we contact if needs change between visits?

Do not treat insurance or benefit questions as confirmed coverage. Note the questions to investigate and ask the insurer or program for its decision. Our [guide to paying for home care](/resources/how-families-pay-for-home-care/) can help organize that separate discussion.

## 6. Decide what to share at the first conversation

For an initial inquiry, a brief description is enough: the general location, the routines that have changed, the help requested, preferred times, and a contact person. Ask how any necessary private information should be shared before sending records.

Keep sensitive details such as door codes, financial account information, and medical documents out of a general website inquiry. Your printed worksheet can stay with the family until you decide which details are needed and who should receive them.

## 7. Finish with one next step and a review date

Choose a next action that is small enough to complete: call a provider, ask a sibling about a recurring visit, or clarify a question with a health professional. Name the person doing it and the date they will report back.

If care begins, use the same worksheet to check whether the original gaps are being addressed. Ask the older adult what feels helpful and what should change. A family planning sheet is a starting point for discussion, not a replacement for the agreed care plan or a professional assessment.
