Falls are common, but they are not an unavoidable part of getting older. Kansas City families can start with two coordinated steps: **ask a healthcare professional about the older adult's personal fall risk, and walk through the home together to find practical hazards.**

This guide provides a room-by-room checklist for that conversation. It does not replace a professional assessment, and no checklist or care service can eliminate every fall.

The timing is especially relevant this month. The National Council on Aging has designated **September 21–25 as Falls Prevention Awareness Week 2026**. Mid-America Regional Council also lists a free Kansas City _A Matter of Balance_ class beginning September 8. Details about that class were checked on September 3, 2026, and should be confirmed with the organizer before attending.

## Start with the older adult's priorities

A home-safety conversation works best when it is done **with** an older adult, not **to** them. Begin by asking where they feel least steady, which routes they use at night, and what changes they would find acceptable.

Avoid turning the walkthrough into an inspection of everything the person is doing “wrong.” A favorite rug, a dim evening routine, or furniture arranged for years may carry practical or emotional importance. Explain the concern, listen to the person's preference, and look for a solution together.

If the family has noticed a new change—such as dizziness, weakness, confusion, difficulty walking, or a recent fall—contact an appropriate healthcare professional. A sudden or severe symptom may require urgent care. Call 911 for an emergency.

## Complete a room-by-room home safety check

Use the routes the person actually travels. Start outside, walk through the main living spaces, and finish with the path between the bed and bathroom. Try the walkthrough during the day and after dark because lighting conditions can reveal different problems.

### Entrances and outdoor walkways

- Look for uneven pavement, loose gravel, leaves, hoses, or cords across the route.
- Check whether steps and changes in level are easy to see.
- Confirm that exterior lights illuminate the lock, threshold, steps, and walkway.
- Make sure frequently used railings feel stable.
- Decide how snow, ice, and wet leaves will be handled before colder weather arrives.
- Keep packages, shoes, and seasonal decorations out of the normal walking path.

Kansas City weather can change quickly in fall. A plan for clearing leaves and dealing with wet or icy surfaces is more useful when responsibilities are assigned before bad weather arrives.

### Stairs and hallways

The CDC recommends handrails on both sides of stairs, good lighting, and keeping floors free of objects that could cause a trip.

- Remove boxes, shoes, pet toys, and electrical cords from the route.
- Replace burned-out bulbs and make switches accessible at both ends of a passage when possible.
- Check that stair edges are visible and floor coverings lie flat.
- Avoid carrying a load that blocks the person's view of the steps.
- Ask a qualified professional to repair loose steps, flooring, or railings.

Do not improvise a weight-bearing grab surface. Towel bars, furniture, and decorative trim may not support a person safely.

### Living room

- Create a wide, direct route between the person's usual chair, doorway, phone, and bathroom.
- Move low tables, footstools, plant stands, and cords away from that route.
- Remove loose rugs or secure them so they cannot slide or curl.
- Check whether chairs are stable and practical for the person to get into and out of.
- Keep commonly used items within comfortable reach to reduce stretching or climbing.
- Notice whether a pet frequently moves through the person's feet.

The goal is not an empty room. It is a familiar room with predictable walking paths and fewer surprises underfoot.

### Bedroom and nighttime route

- Place a lamp or reachable light control near the bed.
- Use nightlights between the bed and bathroom.
- Keep phones, glasses, and other nighttime necessities within easy reach.
- Make sure blankets, clothing, cords, and footwear stay off the floor.
- Avoid relying on unstable furniture for support.
- Check whether the route changes when a closet or bathroom door is open.

If the person gets up frequently at night or feels dizzy when standing, share that information with a healthcare professional rather than treating it only as a lighting problem.

### Bathroom

Bathrooms combine hard surfaces, water, and changes in position. The CDC recommends grab bars inside and next to the tub or shower and beside the toilet.

- Use properly installed grab bars where a qualified professional recommends them.
- Check for water that collects on the floor.
- Use a secure, non-slip surface in the tub or shower.
- Keep towels and toiletries within easy reach.
- Improve nighttime lighting leading into and inside the bathroom.
- Ask an occupational therapist or another qualified professional whether additional equipment or changes would suit the individual.

A suction-cup device or towel rack should not automatically be treated as a structural grab bar. Installation and product choice matter.

### Kitchen and dining area

- Store everyday dishes, food, and appliances between waist and shoulder height when practical.
- Avoid chairs, stools, or boxes as climbing aids.
- Clean spills promptly and address flooring that lifts or shifts.
- Keep the route between food preparation, table, and sink clear.
- Use enough light to see spills, edges, and objects on the floor.
- Sit for tasks when that approach has been recommended and works safely for the person.

## Discuss health and medication factors

Removing a loose rug is useful, but fall risk is not only about the home. The CDC recommends that older adults discuss fall risk with a healthcare provider, review medicines that may cause dizziness or sleepiness, have their eyes checked at least yearly, and participate in activities that improve strength and balance when appropriate.

Write down questions before the next appointment:

1. Has the person fallen, nearly fallen, or begun holding walls or furniture?
2. Do they feel dizzy, unsteady, unusually tired, or lightheaded?
3. Have medications or doses changed recently?
4. Is vision or hearing making the environment harder to interpret?
5. Are shoes supportive and appropriate for the surfaces in the home?
6. Which strength or balance activities are safe for this individual?
7. Would a physical therapist, occupational therapist, pharmacist, vision professional, or hearing professional add useful expertise?

Do not stop or change a medication based on a general checklist. Bring every prescription, over-the-counter medicine, and supplement to the attention of the relevant healthcare or pharmacy professional.

## Make a plan for a fall

Prevention matters, but families also need a response plan. Decide how the person will call for help, who should be contacted, and how responders can enter the home if the person cannot reach the door.

Keep emergency information current and easy to find. Include the person's address, emergency contacts, medications, allergies, healthcare contacts, and any advance-care documents the family has chosen to maintain.

If a fall occurs, avoid assuming the person is uninjured simply because they want to get up. Follow the instructions of emergency or healthcare professionals. Call 911 for an emergency, including serious injury, severe pain, loss of consciousness, new confusion, difficulty breathing, or symptoms that could indicate a stroke. When uncertain about the appropriate response, contact a qualified medical professional.

Tell the person's healthcare provider about a fall or meaningful change in balance. Understanding what happened may reveal a medical, medication, vision, mobility, or environmental factor that needs attention.

## Understand how non-medical care may fit

Home modifications and clinical fall-risk evaluations belong to qualified professionals. Non-medical companion care has a different role.

Depending on the agreed care plan, a companion visit may support:

- A calmer daily routine with less pressure to rush.
- Meal preparation and light household routines.
- Keeping agreed walking routes free of everyday clutter during a visit.
- Accompaniment for approved activities or errands.
- Conversation and social engagement.
- Communication of observed changes or concerns to the designated family contact.

Companion care cannot guarantee fall prevention, provide emergency response, diagnose a condition, prescribe exercise, review medications clinically, or replace skilled nursing, physical therapy, or occupational therapy.
