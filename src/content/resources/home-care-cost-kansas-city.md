Families comparing **home care costs in Kansas City** need two numbers: a reasonable planning benchmark and a quote based on the care they actually want. CareScout's 2025 survey reported a **$33 median hourly rate for a non-medical caregiver in Missouri**. That figure can help with an early budget, but it is not Blue River Home Care's rate and it is not a guaranteed Kansas City price.

The final cost depends on the schedule, the support requested, provider policies, and what is included in the agreement. A useful first step is to estimate hours around the parts of the week that are hardest for the client and family, then request a written quote.

## Start with a current Missouri benchmark

CareScout surveyed long-term care providers across the country between July and November 2025. Its state table lists Missouri's median non-medical caregiver rate at $33 per hour. The survey also models $6,292 per month and $75,504 per year based on **44 hours of care each week**.

Those monthly and annual figures are not a minimum commitment. They are standardized examples that make states and care types easier to compare. Many families begin by discussing a smaller schedule centered on specific routines.

You may also see lower rates on caregiver marketplaces. Those figures can be useful, but they are not necessarily equivalent to a private-pay agency quote. Check the date, whether the number is a starting or median rate, whether it represents an individual or an agency, and which services or administrative costs are included.

## Turn an hourly rate into a monthly budget

Use this formula for a first estimate:

**Hourly rate × hours per week × 4.33 = estimated monthly cost**

At the $33 Missouri benchmark, the math looks like this:

| Example schedule  | Weekly estimate | Average monthly estimate |
| ----------------- | --------------: | -----------------------: |
| 8 hours per week  |            $264 |                   $1,143 |
| 12 hours per week |            $396 |                   $1,715 |
| 20 hours per week |            $660 |                   $2,858 |
| 30 hours per week |            $990 |                   $4,287 |
| 44 hours per week |          $1,452 |                   $6,287 |

These are planning examples, rounded to the nearest dollar. They do not include possible minimum shifts, different rates for certain schedules, holidays, mileage policies, or other provider-specific terms.

The best schedule is not automatically the largest one. Start with the real pressure points: perhaps mornings are difficult, a spouse needs a dependable afternoon break, or adult children cannot cover meal preparation and household routines during the workweek.

## Understand what changes the price

### The number and length of visits

A provider may require a minimum number of hours per visit. Two short visits can also be priced differently from one longer visit because travel and scheduling happen twice. Ask how minimums work before comparing totals.

### The support in the care plan

Companionship, meal help, laundry, light cleaning, errands, transfers, and personal routines do not create identical plans. Describe the current routine accurately so the provider can determine whether its caregivers and service model fit.

### Timing and availability

Weekday daytime hours may be easier to staff than evenings, weekends, holidays, or a schedule that changes frequently. Blue River's current availability also depends on caregiver staffing and travel distance from its Belton office.

### Changes after care begins

A schedule that works today may need to change later. Ask how often the care plan is reviewed, how rate changes are communicated, and what happens if the family wants to add, reduce, or move hours.

## Build a budget from a real week

An estimate is more useful when it reflects a real routine. Instead of choosing an arbitrary number of hours, map one ordinary week.

Begin with the times when a family member is already available and wants to continue helping. Then mark the uncovered periods. Write the specific need beside each one: morning personal routines, lunch preparation, laundry, companionship, transportation, or an afternoon when a spouse needs to leave the house.

For example, a family might identify three priorities:

- Four hours on Monday for meals, laundry, and companionship.
- Four hours on Wednesday so a spouse can attend appointments and errands.
- Four hours on Friday to support the end-of-week routine.

That produces a 12-hour weekly starting point. At the $33 Missouri benchmark, the planning estimate is $396 per week or about $1,715 in an average month. The agency may quote a different rate or require a different visit structure, but the family now has a concrete schedule to discuss.

Build a second estimate for a harder week. If the regular family caregiver travels, works overtime, or becomes ill, what additional hours would be necessary? This is not a commitment to buy those hours. It shows whether the household has enough financial and practical flexibility when the usual plan changes.

### Separate care costs from existing home costs

Remaining at home means the mortgage or rent, taxes, utilities, groceries, maintenance, and transportation generally continue. Do not compare only an hourly home-care bill with an assisted-living monthly price. If the family is weighing both options, use the [home care versus assisted living guide](/resources/home-care-vs-assisted-living-kansas-city/) to list what each price includes.

### Decide who manages the budget

Identify the person who will review invoices, communicate schedule changes, and track any insurance or benefit paperwork. When several relatives contribute, agree in advance on the amount, timing, and process for unexpected costs. A clear administrative plan prevents an urgent care decision from becoming a recurring family dispute.

### Revisit the estimate

Review the first month against the actual routine. Were the scheduled hours used where they mattered most? Did the family add unpaid coverage around the visits? Did cancellations or transportation affect the total? The answers can help the client and family request a more sustainable plan.

Keep the benchmark date beside the estimate. Care prices change, and an older survey should not be treated as a permanent local rate. Update the calculation when the provider changes its price, the care plan adds hours, or the family begins comparing a different type of care. A budget that is revisited deliberately is more useful than a precise spreadsheet built on an outdated assumption.

Finally, leave room for ordinary household variation. Some months contain five occurrences of a weekday, a holiday, or an extra family absence. The 4.33 multiplier creates an average month; an actual invoice may be higher or lower depending on the scheduled calendar and provider terms.

## Compare an agency with an independent caregiver

The hourly number is only one part of the comparison.

When considering an agency, ask who employs the caregiver, who handles scheduling and payroll, how supervision works, what happens when the regular caregiver is unavailable, and which insurance or screening practices apply. When considering a direct hire, ask who becomes responsible for recruiting, taxes, documentation, backup coverage, and resolving performance concerns.

Neither model is automatically right for every family. Compare the complete responsibility and risk—not just the number beside “hourly rate.” Our guide to [choosing a Kansas City home care agency](/resources/choose-home-care-agency-kansas-city/) provides a detailed checklist.

## Know what Medicare generally covers

Medicare distinguishes covered home health services from ongoing non-medical help.

According to Medicare, covered home health may include intermittent skilled nursing, therapy, medical social services, and limited home health aide care when eligibility conditions are met. Medicare says it does not cover 24-hour care at home, meal delivery, homemaker services unrelated to a covered care plan, or custodial or personal care when that is the only care needed.

That is why Blue River describes its service as primarily **private-pay, non-medical home care**. A family may still want to investigate a long-term care insurance policy, Veterans or survivor benefits, Masonic assistance, Missouri programs, or another source. The insurer or program—not Blue River—decides eligibility and payment.

Read [how families can pay for home care](/resources/how-families-pay-for-home-care/) for the questions to take to each program.

## Ask for a complete quote

Before agreeing to care, ask:

1. What is the hourly rate for the schedule we described?
2. Is there a minimum visit length or weekly minimum?
3. Are evenings, weekends, or holidays priced differently?
4. Are transportation or mileage costs separate?
5. What is included in the rate?
6. How are schedule changes, cancellations, and rate increases handled?
7. When will we receive a written agreement and care plan?
